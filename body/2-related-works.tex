This chapter reviews four categories of techniques closely related to this study:
(1) diffusion models,
(2) diffusion-based monocular depth estimation,
(3) single-image 3D scene reconstruction and generation,
and (4) diffusion-based novel view synthesis and video generation.

\section{Diffusion Models}

The diffusion model represents a major recent breakthrough in generative modeling,
whose core idea originates from the diffusion process in non-equilibrium thermodynamics.
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), proposed by Ho et al. \cite{ddpm},
define the forward noising process as a Markov chain
that gradually transforms the data distribution into Gaussian noise,
and train a neural network to learn the reverse denoising process,
which starts from pure noise and progressively recovers real samples, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ddpmrw}.
DDPM substantially surpassed the contemporaneous Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) in image generation quality,
establishing diffusion models as the dominant generative architecture.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{DDPM.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the DDPM forward noising and reverse denoising processes. The dashed arrows denote the forward process,
  which gradually adds Gaussian noise to the real data $x_0$ until pure noise $x_T$ is reached;
  the solid arrows denote the reverse process, in which a neural network learns to progressively denoise $x_T$ back to $x_0$.}
  \label{fig:ddpmrw}
\end{figure}

However, the Markov-chain sampling procedure of DDPM requires hundreds to thousands of denoising steps to generate high-quality samples,
and the resulting slow inference makes it difficult to apply in practical scenarios.
Song et al. proposed Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) \cite{ddim},
which redefine the reverse sampling steps as a non-Markovian process:
the forward process of DDIM shares the same marginal distributions as that of DDPM,
so the training results of DDPM can be reused directly,
while its reverse sampling adopts a deterministic formulation
that enables high-quality generation with only a few dozen steps,
accelerating inference by a factor of 10 to 50, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ddimrw}.
Moreover, the deterministic sampling of DDIM makes latent-space interpolation and inversion possible,
providing a key technical foundation for subsequent research on diffusion-based image editing, depth estimation, and video generation (including Marigold-DC adopted in this study).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{DDIM.png}
  \caption{Illustration of DDIM: DDIM performs reverse sampling with a non-Markovian deterministic formulation that steps directly across timesteps,
  greatly accelerating inference.}
  \label{fig:ddimrw}
\end{figure}

Although DDIM improves the computational speed of the model,
the computational cost of operating on high-resolution images in pixel space remains extremely high.
Rombach et al. proposed Latent Diffusion Models (LDM) \cite{ldm},
which first compress an image into a low-dimensional latent space with an autoencoder,
then perform the diffusion process in the latent space, and finally restore the result to pixel space with a decoder after inference,
substantially reducing the computational burden of training and inference, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ldmrw}.
When trained with text-conditioning on the large-scale image--text dataset LAION-5B \cite{LAION-5B},
LDM becomes the widely used Stable Diffusion model,
whose rich accumulated visual semantic priors form an important foundation for numerous downstream tasks
(including depth estimation, novel view synthesis, and video generation);
Marigold \cite{marigold}, Marigold-DC \cite{marigolddc}, %
Depth Anything V3 \cite{depthanything}, and Uni3C \cite{uni3c} adopted in this study are all built upon this foundation \cite{ldm}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{LDM.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the LDM architecture. The input image is compressed into a low-dimensional latent space by the encoder, the diffusion and denoising processes are performed in the latent space, and the decoder finally restores the result to pixel space, greatly reducing the computational cost of operating directly in pixel space. Conditioning signals (text, images, etc.) are injected into the denoising network via cross-attention.}
  \label{fig:ldmrw}
\end{figure}

\section{Diffusion-Based Monocular Depth Estimation}

Monocular depth estimation takes a single image as input
and predicts the relative or absolute depth value of every pixel.
However, a single image inherently lacks disparity information,
and the same 2D image can correspond to infinitely many 3D scene configurations,
which is known as the depth-ambiguity problem.
When capturing a photograph, the camera projects the 3D world onto a 2D plane;
this process is irreversible---the distance, size, and occlusion relations of objects cannot be recovered directly from a single image.
With only one image as input,
a model can rely solely on the geometric priors
and scene statistics learned from its training data to infer depth.
This ill-posed nature keeps monocular depth estimation one of the core challenges in computer vision to this day,
and makes it highly sensitive to the scene distribution of the training data---
a model that performs well in specific scenes often struggles to generalize to new domains with markedly different styles.

Early supervised methods (e.g., DPT \cite{dpt} and AdaBins \cite{adabins}) were trained on real-scene photographs such as NYU Depth \cite{nyudepth} and KITTI \cite{kitti},
achieving excellent performance in controlled scenes,
but the fundamental gap between their training data and the visual characteristics of TCP
makes it difficult for them to produce reliable estimates for images dominated by white space and brushstrokes,
and TCP-specific depth datasets are extremely scarce, precluding effective training of existing models.

Marigold \cite{marigold} pioneered a new paradigm of applying diffusion models to depth estimation.
Its core idea is to start from a pretrained text-to-image latent diffusion model (Stable Diffusion v2) \cite{ldm},
directly inheriting the rich visual priors learned on the large-scale image--text dataset LAION-5B \cite{LAION-5B},
and to replace conventional supervised regression with a denoising process.
Compared with supervised methods that require large-scale real-scene depth-annotated datasets for training from scratch,
Marigold only requires fine-tuning on a small amount of synthetic-scene depth data (Hypersim and Virtual KITTI)
to exploit the cross-scene visual priors accumulated during pretraining
and generate an affine-invariant depth map for an arbitrary input image;
its fine-tuning architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:marigoldrw}.
Affine invariance means that the prediction $\hat{D}$ and the actual depth $D^*$
are related by an unknown scale and shift:
$\hat{D} = s \cdot D^* + b$ (with $s, b$ unknown),
so the model only guarantees the relative depth ordering among pixels and cannot directly output absolute depth values in meters.
Owing to the rich visual priors accumulated by the diffusion model, Marigold achieves better zero-shot generalization across domains than supervised methods.
However, the original Marigold outputs only affine-invariant relative depth
and provides no interface for injecting external depth constraints at inference time;
once the estimate deviates, the user has no targeted means of correction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{marigold_pipeline.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the Marigold fine-tuning pipeline.
  Top path: the RGB image $\mathbf{x}$ is compressed by the frozen latent encoder into the conditioning code $\mathbf{z}^{(\mathbf{x})}$.
  Middle path: the depth map $\mathbf{d}$ is likewise compressed by the frozen encoder,
  then combined with sampled noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ according to the timestep $t$ to obtain the noisy depth latent $\mathbf{z}_t^{(\mathbf{d})}$.
  The latents of the two paths are concatenated and fed into the U-Net to predict the noise $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$,
  and the training objective is to minimize $\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|^2$.}
  \label{fig:marigoldrw}
\end{figure}

Depth Anything V3 \cite{depthanything} is trained on large-scale diverse data
and integrates multi-view geometric consistency supervision,
achieving significant improvements in both zero-shot generalization and metric depth accuracy
and exhibiting strong adaptability to a wide range of image styles;
its architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:da3rw}.
However, like the supervised methods, its architecture provides no sparse depth guidance interface
and cannot accept user-specified local depth corrections at inference time.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{da3_pipeline.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the Depth Anything V3 architecture.
  The input image passes through Image Patch Embed into a Single Transformer built on a Vanilla DINOv2 \cite{dinov2} backbone,
  and a Dual-DPT Head then simultaneously outputs the depth map (Depth), the ray directions (Ray), and the 3D point coordinates (Points),
  with an optional camera-intrinsics token (Camera) to improve estimation accuracy.}
  \label{fig:da3rw}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{marigoldDC_pipeline}
  \caption{The guided inference pipeline of Marigold-DC.
  Top half: the inference path, in which the RGB image $\mathbf{x}$ is encoded by the latent encoder into $\mathbf{z}^{(\mathbf{x})}$,
  the depth latent $\mathbf{z}_t^{(\mathbf{d})}$ is concatenated with it and fed into the U-Net for denoising,
  the clean latent feature $\mathbf{z}_0^{(\mathbf{d})}$ is obtained after all denoising steps are completed,
  and the VAE decoder $\mathcal{D}(\cdot)$ then decodes it into the affine-invariant depth map $\hat{\mathbf{d}}^{(m)}$.
  Bottom half: the sparse guidance path, in which the Tweedie formula estimates the current intermediate depth
  $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_t^{(m)}$ at each denoising step, its loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to the sparse control points $\mathbf{c}$ is computed,
  and the gradient is backpropagated to the latent to impose geometric constraints.
  Green locks denote frozen pretrained parameters; the flame symbol denotes the learnable scale and shift ($\hat{a}, \hat{b}$).}
  \label{fig:marigolddc}
\end{figure}

Marigold-DC \cite{marigolddc} introduces a sparse depth guidance mechanism (sparse depth completion) on top of Marigold.
The user only needs to provide depth values at a small number of pixel positions as control points,
and the model uses these sparse constraints to guide the generation of the entire depth field during the denoising iterations,
steering the zero-shot estimate toward the user's intent without any retraining.
This mechanism is particularly critical for scenarios where training data are scarce and local corrective intervention is required (such as TCP depth estimation),
and is the core reason this study selects it as the depth estimation backbone.
However, Marigold-DC is designed for real-scene photographs, and its loss function contains only a geometric-accuracy constraint.
When the whitespace semantics, brushstroke structure, and abstract spatial expression unique to TCP are taken into account,
directly applying it to TCP depth estimation tends to produce depth distributions inconsistent with the painting's intent.
Moreover, how to systematically guide users in deciding the positions and depth values of the control points is also an important issue in practical applications.
This study proposes improvements for these problems: the control-point annotation procedure is detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:dc}, and the loss function design is detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:loss}.
The sparse guidance pipeline of Marigold-DC is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:marigolddc}.

\section{Single-Image 3D Scene Reconstruction and Generation}

Reconstructing or generating a 3D scene from a single image is a long-standing challenge in computer vision.
Recent developments in diffusion models have significantly advanced research in this direction.

Zero-1-to-3 \cite{zero123} takes a single object image as input
and generates novel-view images at arbitrary viewpoints through a viewpoint-conditioned diffusion model,
achieving zero-shot single-image 3D object synthesis,
as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:zero123rw}.
The method demonstrates the potential of diffusion models for 3D understanding,
but it primarily targets individual objects and is difficult to extend directly to scene-level tasks with complex backgrounds.

  \caption{Illustration of Zero-1-to-3. (Left) The viewpoint-conditioned diffusion model architecture, which generates the image at the target viewpoint conditioned on the input image and the relative viewpoint offset ($\Delta R, \Delta T$); (right) multi-view synthesis results, in which a sequence of novel-view images under continuously rotating viewpoints is generated from a single input image.}
  \label{fig:zero123rw}
\end{figure}

Difix3D+ \cite{difix3d} proposes repairing geometric artifacts in 3D reconstruction with a single-step diffusion model,
post-processing the floater noise and holes produced by reconstruction methods such as NeRF and 3DGS under incomplete data or occlusion,
thereby improving reconstruction quality without retraining, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:difix3drw}.

WonderWorld \cite{wonderworld} takes a single image as input
and combines depth estimation with Gaussian Splatting
to let users interactively explore and extend 3D scenes,
generating an immersive scene representation with a sense of parallax.
This work shows that the tight integration of depth estimation and scene generation is a feasible path toward interactive 3D presentation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:wonderworldrw}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Difix3Dplus.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the Difix3D+ repair pipeline. The floater noise and hole regions remaining after NeRF or 3DGS reconstruction are post-processed and repaired by a single-step diffusion model, improving geometric quality without retraining the reconstruction model.}
  \label{fig:difix3drw}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{wonderworld.png}
  \caption{Illustration of interactive scene extension in WonderWorld. Starting from a single input image and combining depth estimation with Gaussian Splatting, the user can interactively explore and extend the 3D scene outward, generating an immersive scene representation with a sense of parallax.}
  \label{fig:wonderworldrw}
\end{figure}

Fu et al. \cite{highreliefbrush} first proposed a method for generating a high-relief 3D model from a single ink wash painting,
an early representative work dedicated to 2.5D geometric reconstruction of TCP.
Its core idea is to regard an ink wash painting as the superposition of a large number of semi-transparent brushstrokes,
preserving the color, texture, and stroke shape of every brushstroke to maximally retain the artistic style of the original painting.
To this end, the method combines layer decomposition with brushstroke segmentation
to infer the occlusion relations during relief composition and to preserve the stroke pressure on the relief surface,
faithfully extracting every brushstroke of the painting;
finally, an interactive interface is used to manually adjust the inflation of each brushstroke,
which, together with a displacement map, completes the high-relief presentation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:highreliefrw}.
In practice, however, this method remains highly dependent on manual intervention:
the layer decomposition adopts an optimization algorithm based on a convex-hull assumption in RGB geometric space,
requiring the user to manually specify the number and order of the palette colors, where improper settings easily lead to color distortion;
segmenting at the granularity of individual brushstrokes faithfully preserves the painting style,
yet forces the subsequent composition of brushstrokes in 3D space to consider far more orderings, increasing the manual workload;
the relief generation stage further requires the user to mark the slope and curvature of each brushstroke region one by one with arrows,
so the overall workflow relies on extensive manual operations and is difficult to generalize to complex paintings.
This study replaces rule-based geometric assumptions with the visual priors of diffusion models
and replaces per-brushstroke manual marking with sparse depth control points,
so that the depth estimates conform to the painting's spatial semantics and the user's intent while greatly reducing the annotation burden.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{high_relief.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the High Relief from Brush Painting system architecture \cite{highreliefbrush}.
  The input ink wash painting undergoes layer decomposition and brushstroke segmentation, inflation and a displacement map are applied to each brushstroke,
  and the results are finally merged into a textured 2.5D high-relief model.}
  \label{fig:highreliefrw}
\end{figure}

Zero-1-to-3 \cite{zero123}, Difix3D+ \cite{difix3d}, and WonderWorld \cite{wonderworld} all take real-scene photographs or synthetic 3D objects
as their primary training and evaluation targets and are unsuitable for direct application to TCP:
Zero-1-to-3 is centered on individual objects, whereas TCP scenes possess no extractable object-level geometric structure;
Difix3D+ relies on the output of an existing 3D reconstruction, whereas TCP lacks sufficient multi-view data to support a reconstruction pipeline;
WonderWorld drives scene extension with depth estimation, but its depth module is trained on real photographs,
with limited understanding of the spatial semantics of whitespace composition and abstract brushstrokes, and its depth estimation errors directly degrade the quality of the subsequent scenes.
Moreover, none of these three methods provides a way for the user to intervene in the depth estimation;
once the model misjudges a TCP image, no targeted local correction is possible.
To address these difficulties, this study builds on Marigold-DC,
introduces a human-in-the-loop sparse depth control point annotation mechanism,
and combines it with \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss} designed specifically for TCP,
so that the depth estimates conform to the visual semantics of the painting while preserving the user's active control over key regions,
thereby generating convincing 2.5D videos even under the extreme scarcity of real 3D data.

\section{Diffusion-Based Novel View Synthesis and Video Generation}

The combination of novel view synthesis and video generation
provides a feasible framework for generating videos with a sense of camera motion from a single image.

ViewCrafter \cite{viewcrafter} conditions on a single image and point-cloud renderings
and employs a video diffusion model to generate high-fidelity novel view synthesis videos.
Its key contribution lies in providing geometric priors through point-cloud rendering,
driving the diffusion model to maintain the 3D consistency of the scene during denoising
while filling the occluded regions exposed by viewpoint shifts;
this work shows that point clouds serve as effective geometric guidance for driving video diffusion models to generate 3D-consistent videos.
The ViewCrafter architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:viewcrafterrw}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{ViewCrafter.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the ViewCrafter pipeline. Conditioned on a single reference image and point-cloud renderings, a video diffusion model synthesizes novel-view videos while maintaining 3D geometric consistency and fills the occluded regions.}
  \label{fig:viewcrafterrw}
\end{figure}

TrajectoryCrafter \cite{trajectorycrafter} takes a diffusion model as its core
and redirects the camera path of a monocular video to a user-specified target trajectory,
synthesizing videos captured along the new trajectory with realistic parallax,
achieving significant progress in free-viewpoint video generation, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:trajcrafterrw}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{CameraTrajectory.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the TrajectoryCrafter pipeline. Conditioned on a monocular video and a user-specified target camera trajectory, a diffusion model synthesizes novel-view videos captured along the new trajectory with realistic parallax.}
  \label{fig:trajcrafterrw}
\end{figure}

Uni3C \cite{uni3c} proposes a unified 3D-enhanced control framework
that simultaneously employs precise 3D camera trajectories and human motion as guidance conditions for a video diffusion model.
Its core module, PCDController, encodes point-cloud renderings into geometric guidance signals
and imposes strict geometric constraints during denoising, ensuring that the viewpoint motion of the generated video is consistent with the input trajectory.
This study adopts the PCDController of Uni3C as the final video generation module,
using the point-cloud video to guide the video diffusion model in filling occlusion holes while maintaining the overall style of TCP.
In addition, this study makes corresponding TCP-specific modifications,
which are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:video}.
The overall architecture of Uni3C is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:uni3cpipeline}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{uni3c_pipeline.png}
  \caption{Illustration of the Uni3C video generation architecture. The point-cloud rendered video is encoded by the 3D-VAE of Wan2.1 into the point latent ($c_{\mathrm{pcd}}$), and a six-channel Pl\"ucker ray embedding encodes the 3D position and ray direction of each pixel; these serve as the two guidance signals of PCDController. The visual features of the first frame are extracted by Image CLIP and the Wan-Encoder and injected, together with the text prompt, into the denoising process of the video diffusion model (Wan-DiT). After projection through zero-initialized linear layers, PCDController injects the geometric signals into the first 20 layers of the Wan-I2V denoising process via element-wise addition, ensuring that the camera trajectory of the generated video is consistent with the point-cloud renderings; finally, the Wan-Decoder decodes and outputs the complete 2.5D video.}
  \label{fig:uni3cpipeline}
\end{figure}

Although the above methods have made important progress in point-cloud-guided video generation,
they are all trained primarily on real-scene imagery and lack designs targeting the special visual characteristics of TCP.
The depth estimation module of ViewCrafter has limited understanding of the spatial semantics of whitespace composition and abstract brushstrokes;
direct application easily produces erroneous depth distributions in whitespace regions, which in turn leads to point-cloud distortion and video jitter.
TrajectoryCrafter takes a monocular video as input and requires temporal information as a prerequisite,
so it cannot generate camera motion directly from a single static TCP image; it is likewise trained on real-scene videos
and lacks the capability to realize the visual style of TCP.
Uni3C provides a more precise geometric guidance mechanism, yet it is likewise not designed for TCP style preservation,
and its denoising process may oversmooth brushstroke textures or produce hallucinations.
Building on these frameworks, this study proposes three TCP-specific improvements:
(1) \textbf{human-in-the-loop depth annotation}: a rough depth map is first obtained with Depth Anything V2 \cite{depthanythingv2} for human inspection,
the user then annotates the misjudged regions with sparse control points, and Marigold-DC is executed with these control points as guidance,
injecting human spatial intent into the depth estimation process and replacing the non-intervenable automatic depth estimation of the above frameworks;
(2) \textbf{a TCP-specific loss function}: we design \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss},
which covers whitespace regularization and a brushstroke-aware smoothness loss,
translating the visual semantics unique to TCP into differentiable optimization objectives so that the depth map follows the painting's intent rather than real-scene geometry;
(3) \textbf{style-preserving video generation}: the PCDController of Uni3C replaces the video diffusion control module of ViewCrafter,
driving the video diffusion model under stricter geometric constraints while exploiting its diffusion priors to maintain the ink-and-brush style of TCP when filling occlusion holes.
The core difficulty of these improvements lies in the extreme scarcity of TCP depth annotation data, which precludes direct supervised training;
this study therefore adopts a strategy that combines human-in-the-loop collaboration with diffusion priors, producing depth estimates and 2.5D videos consistent with the painting's intent even under limited data.
