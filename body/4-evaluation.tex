This chapter presents an experimental evaluation of the TCP-Uni3C pipeline proposed in Chapter 3.
Section~\ref{sec:exp_setup} describes the experimental environment, test materials, and baselines;
Section~\ref{sec:exp_depth} evaluates the quality of depth estimation,
including a qualitative comparison with existing methods and an ablation study of the individual loss terms of \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss};
Section~\ref{sec:exp_video} presents the final 2.5D video generation results
and analyzes the effect of camera parameter settings on generation quality;
Section~\ref{sec:exp_discussion} discusses the scope of applicability and current limitations of the proposed method.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Hardware and Software Environment}

% TODO: adjust according to the actual environment
All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 (24 GB VRAM),
running Ubuntu 22.04.
The depth estimation module is implemented in PyTorch;
point-cloud unprojection and video generation are integrated through a ComfyUI workflow that combines the Depth Anything V3 and Uni3C inference pipelines.

\subsection{Baselines}

To validate the contribution of each module in this study, the experiments include the following baselines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Depth estimation}:
    direct inference with Depth Anything V2 serves as the baseline,
    compared against the full configuration of this study (sparse control points + \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss}).
  \item \textbf{Video generation}:
    the original Uni3C (with its built-in Depth-Pro depth estimation) serves as the baseline,
    compared against TCP-Uni3C (guided by the point cloud from the refined depth).
\end{itemize}

\section{Depth Estimation Results}
\label{sec:exp_depth}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Qualitative Comparison with Existing Methods}

Figure~\ref{fig:depth_comparison} compares the results of different depth estimation methods on the test paintings.
The baseline method exhibits the typical depth misjudgments on TCP:
the whitespace background is assigned intermediate depth values, lacking hierarchical separation from the main subject,
and the overall depth field tends to be flat.
After refinement with sparse control points and \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss},
the white-space regions are indeed pushed to the farthest end,
the subject contours emerge clearly along the brushstroke boundaries,
and the foreground--background depth layering is consistent with the spatial semantics of the painting.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{depth_comparison}
  \caption{Qualitative comparison of different depth estimation methods.
    Three groups of paintings are shown;
    each group, from left to right: the input image, the inference result of Depth Anything V2\cite{depthanythingv2},
    and the refined depth map of this study (sparse control points + \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss});
    in the depth maps, bright regions are near and dark regions are far.
    The baseline misjudges the whitespace background as mid-distance (shown in gray),
    whereas our method correctly pushes the white space to the far end (shown in black), yielding clear separation between subject and background.}
  \label{fig:depth_comparison}

\subsection{Ablation Study of ChinesePaintingLoss}

To quantify the contribution of each loss term, we accumulate the loss functions one by one and observe the changes in depth map quality:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Only $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}}$
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}}$
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{white}}$
        (the full \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss})
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp_ablation} presents the depth estimation results under the three settings.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{exp_ablation}
  \caption{Results of the \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss} ablation study.
    Three groups of paintings are shown;
    each group, from left to right: the input image, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}}$ only,
    $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}}$,
    and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{white}}$ (the full \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss});
    in the depth maps, bright regions are near and dark regions are far.
    As the loss terms are progressively accumulated, the depth discontinuities on both sides of the brushstrokes are gradually eliminated,
    the whitespace background is pushed toward the far end, and the layering between subject and background becomes increasingly distinct.}
  \label{fig:exp_ablation}
\end{figure}

With $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{metric}}$ alone,
the depth values at the control-point locations successfully converge to the user annotations, and the foreground and background are roughly separated;
however, the regions beyond the control points lack structural constraints,
unnatural depth discontinuities tend to appear on both sides of dense-ink brushstrokes, and the depth within the subject is insufficiently smooth.
After adding $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}}$,
the edge weight automatically reduces the smoothness penalty along brushstroke contours while maintaining smoothing pressure elsewhere,
making the depth within the subject more continuous and markedly reducing brushstroke-induced discontinuities.
Finally, adding $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{white}}$ to form the full \textit{ChinesePaintingLoss}
pushes the large whitespace background further toward the farthest end,
sharpening the depth contrast between subject and background,
in accordance with the visual semantics of TCP, in which white space conveys deep, distant space.

\section{2.5D Video Generation Results}

\subsection{Comparison with the Original Uni3C}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp_uni3c} compares the generation results of the original Uni3C and TCP-Uni3C.
The original Uni3C is driven by the depth estimated with its built-in Depth-Pro;
since accurate depth cannot be obtained, its generation results are unpredictable
and may exhibit geometric distortions or a lack of spatial layering.
TCP-Uni3C guides the generation with the point cloud unprojected from the refined depth;
a clear parallax displacement is visible between the foreground subject and the background,
and the frames convey a convincing sense of spatial depth.

\subsection{Effect of Camera Parameters}

The camera and generation settings in Chapter 3 involve several adjustable parameters:
the scaling factor of the initial camera distance, the vertical offset of the camera, the angular amplitude of the circular trajectory,
and the positive and negative text prompts fed to the video diffusion model.
Taking the lotus painting as an example, this section varies each parameter in turn starting from the baseline setting
to observe its effect on generation quality; the results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure}.

The baseline setting uses a camera-distance scaling factor of $0.85$, a vertical offset of $0.01$,
and an angular amplitude of $15^\circ$,
together with the TCP-specific positive and negative text prompts designed in Chapter 3;
the generated result exhibits smooth parallax and preserves the style of the original painting (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure}, TCP-Uni3C).
The modifications and corresponding results of the three contrast settings are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Improper camera distance}:
        the distance scaling factor is changed from $0.85$ to $1.0$;
        because the camera is farther from the point cloud, the proportion of background not covered by the point cloud increases,
        and ink regions such as the lotus leaves exhibit expansion distortion and loss of brushstroke detail (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure}, $\Delta$ Distance).
  \item \textbf{Improper angular amplitude}:
        the amplitude is changed to $20^\circ$;
        because the excessive viewpoint shift drastically enlarges the hole area,
        the generated result shows pronounced geometric distortion and content hallucination,
        such as petals appearing out of nowhere and misshapen lotus leaves (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure}, $\Delta$ Amplitude).
  \item \textbf{Text prompt setting}:
        the positive prompt is replaced with \textit{``This video features a lotus''} and the negative prompt is left empty,
        a configuration similar to that adopted in Uni3C;
        lacking the semantic constraints of \textit{camera motion} and \textit{static scene} as well as the suppression provided by the negative prompt,
        colored stripes and noise patterns absent from the original painting appear at the frame edges (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure}, $\Delta$ Test prompt).
\end{enumerate}

These results validate the necessity of the design decisions made in Chapter 3:
shortening the camera distance and capping the angular amplitude
keep the regions to be filled within a range that the diffusion model can complete stably,
while the TCP-specific text prompts ensure that the filled content conforms to the semantic expectation of a static scene.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:exp_discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Scope of Applicability}

The proposed method performs best on ink wash TCPs with clear subject contours, distinct boundaries between objects, and a moderate proportion of white space,
such as bird-and-flower paintings composed of a single subject against large areas of white space.
In such paintings, the brushstroke boundaries can be effectively identified by $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{brush}}$,
and the white-space regions can be correctly pushed to the far end by $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{white}}$;
with only a few control points, a depth field consistent with the spatial semantics can be reconstructed,
which in turn yields a 2.5D video with natural parallax.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Depth ambiguity in overlapping brushstrokes.}
The manual annotation in this study operates at the pixel level; each control point can assign only a single depth value to a single pixel location.
However, ink wash painting often expresses the interleaved spatial relations of multiple objects through overlapping brushstrokes---
the ink of foreground and background objects merges within the same region through ink wash blending, producing a nearly translucent visual effect.
In this case, a single pixel actually carries the depth information of multiple objects simultaneously,
whereas the proposed method lacks a mechanism to decompose the image into multiple depth layers;
regardless of the value assigned by a control point, such a region can only converge to a single depth,
making a convincing depth estimate difficult to obtain.

\textbf{Degraded generation quality for large-area filling.}
The final video is generated by a video diffusion model,
and both the occlusion holes exposed by viewpoint shifts and the background regions lacking point-cloud coverage must be filled by the model itself.
When such regions to be filled become too large,
the proportion of the frame governed by the geometric constraints of the point cloud decreases accordingly,
the uncertainty of the generated result rises,
and completions inconsistent with the style of the original painting may appear, degrading the video quality below expectations.

Such fabricated completions correspond to what the diffusion model literature terms the hallucination problem,
whose causes have been explained by several recent studies.
Aithal et al.~\cite{diffhallucination} demonstrate that diffusion models can generate samples lying entirely outside the support of the training data distribution:
neural networks struggle to fit the true but discontinuous score function,
and their smooth approximation causes the model to interpolate between neighboring data modes,
producing content that never appears in the training data;
moreover, such hallucinated samples are accompanied by a marked increase in variance during the final stage of sampling,
echoing our observation that a reduced proportion of geometric constraints raises generation uncertainty.
Regarding conditional generation,
Kim et al.~\cite{structhallucination} confirm that when the conditioning image contains out-of-distribution (OOD) local regions,
diffusion models generate fabricated structural hallucinations at the corresponding locations;
Wang et al.~\cite{asuka} likewise point out that when diffusion inpainting models fill large-area masks,
they tend to generate objects inconsistent with the surrounding image even when the unmasked regions serve as context.
This is also the main reason why Chapter 3 caps the angular amplitude of the camera trajectory at $15^\circ$;
the cases in Figure~\ref{fig:exp_failure} where the amplitude exceeds the cap or the camera distance is not shortened
are concrete instances of this phenomenon.
